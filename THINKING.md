# Thinking before the script

Written before the script of this version, and kept beside it.

## First principles

A moat is whatever lets a company keep earning more than its cost of capital for years while competitors try to take
it away: Buffett's "enduring moat", Helmer's "persistent differential returns". Returns persist only if a rival
cannot reproduce the thing that earns them at the same cost. So for every source of a moat the question is how much
of it is code, and how much is something code cannot make: a permission (a licence, a contract, a party that can be
held liable), physical capacity (power, land, machines and the years to build them), a connection to people who are
already there, and the customer's own relationship and history with the company. What must be measured: how fast
writing software and doing agent work is getting cheaper (price per token, cost per task at a fixed score, the
length of task an agent can finish), whether rebuilds of existing products happen and get used, and whether the
companies whose product is mostly code are losing customers or growth.

## In the limit

Push the trend to its end: code costs nothing and agents do whole jobs alone. Christensen's law of conservation of
attractive profits says that when one stage of the value chain becomes a commodity, the chance of attractive profits
usually moves to an adjacent stage. Agency, the noticing, deciding and following up that services sell, is the next layer, and it is
devalued from both sides: the seller's agents do the work, and the buyer's agents compare and switch, so the
customer who never cancels stops being a moat. What is left, I argue, is what agency does not seem able to produce
on demand: permission, physical capacity and connection, which is Levi's phrase "connected permitted capacity". The measurements thin out
here (METR's task suite cannot measure much past sixteen hours), so the limit is argued, and the video marks every
step of it as a guess.

## The strongest counterargument

Incumbents are fine. ServiceNow's subscription revenue grew 21% and Atlassian's total revenue 24% in their latest
quarters;
HubSpot still keeps more revenue from existing customers than it loses. Switching costs, distribution, trust,
compliance and brand matter far more than code, and the rebuilds are demonstrations: Apple shut Beeper Mini out
within days, and two days after Cloudflare's one-week rebuild, the CEO of a rival said he had found seven security
flaws in it. Cheaper code also
means more software (Jevons), so software companies grow with it. On this view "code is cheap" changes little.

Answer: tested in sections 1, 3, 4, 7 and 8, it partly holds, and the answer is narrowed. The video reports the growth
figures as they are and does not say software companies are dying; it shows what is measurably weakening (HubSpot's
growth from 21% to 17%, 12% guided; net revenue retention from 103% to 101% within 2026; Thomson Reuters falling by
double digits on a plug-in announcement; Slack rationing its API) and shows the two rebuild cases as they ended,
including the block and the security holes. The conclusion, that the customer relationship, the record of decisions,
permission, capacity and connection are what hold, is marked as a guess and called a hypothesis in the open and the
close.
